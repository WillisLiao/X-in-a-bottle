Picked up `NEXT-SESSION-hobbitle-for-real.md`.
Got through the rename, the icon, the sky and the bodies before the session turned into a design conversation that changed the shape of the product.

## The rename

Done everywhere a user can see it: `project.godot`'s app name, the title screen wordmark and its subtitle, both export presets, `deploy.sh`, and the save file.

The owner chose to change the bundle identifier to `com.lull.hobbitle` rather than keep the old one, having been told that it strands the days already built on the device under the old bundle and leaves the old app on the home screen until it is deleted by hand.
`progress.gd` now falls back to reading `user://elvle.cfg` when `user://hobbitle.cfg` is absent, which does nothing on the device but keeps desktop saves alive across the rename.

The class `ElfWorld`, the array `_elves` and most of the prose in the source still say elf, deliberately, with a note at the top of `elf_world.gd` saying so and why.

## The icon

The old one was a neon lightning bolt left over from Lightning in a Bottle, which nobody had looked at in a while.

New one is a round door in a hillside under a low sun, generated by `Bottle3D/tools/icon.gd` rather than being a binary nobody can rebuild.
The construction is deliberately the picker's: a band of sky over a band of ground from the meadow's real palette, with one mark standing on it.

Two things were wrong on the first attempt and worth recording because they are general.
A dark disc inside a cream ring with a dot in the middle reads as an **eye** long before it reads as a door; reversing the values to light-inside-dark fixes it.
And a two-stop gradient from navy to amber passes through the average of the two, which is a dead grey-brown, so the sky is four stops now and keeps its saturation the whole way down.

## The sky, and why it was never the sky

The complaint was "real sky not bright enough, im also not seeing sun or moon".
Both were real bugs and neither had the cause the previous session assumed.

**The sun.** `ProceduralSkyMaterial` does draw a sun for each of the first four `DirectionalLight3D`s, so that assumption was not wrong, but it was never checked and what it actually produces is a wide soft halo, because the disc's size comes from `light_angular_distance`, which defaults to zero.
The property the previous session reached for, `light_angular_size`, does not exist, which is why it threw.
Worse: every directional light gets one, so the *fill* light had been quietly painting a second sun into the sky from 152 degrees behind.

All three lights are now `SKY_MODE_LIGHT_ONLY` and the sun and moon are drawn explicitly as unshaded billboards with a generated soft-disc texture, an albedo pushed past white so they clip through the glow threshold, and fog disabled so forty metres of aerial perspective does not grey out the brightest thing in the frame.
The moon gets three darker blotches, which is a startling amount of recognition for nine lines.

**The sky.** Three separate causes, found by actually capturing rather than reasoning.

1. The camera looks thirty-six degrees down through a thirty-four degree lens, which puts the horizon nineteen degrees above the top of the frame.
   At the default angle and at *every angle a thumb could reach*, none of the visible background was the sky half of the sky material.
   All of it was the ground half, and the ground half was `earth` darkened by a third.
   Mud. Every hour previously spent tuning `sky_top_color` was spent on a colour nobody had ever seen.
2. `glow_bloom` was 0.40. That is not the glow - it is a floor under the threshold, so forty percent of *every* pixel was being blurred and added back over the frame. Invisible when this was glowing objects against black; turns a daylight scene into milk. The sky was never dark, it was washed colourless.
3. `fog_sky_affect` was 0.35, so a third of every sky pixel was the fog's dark blue-grey.

Fixes: the lower hemisphere now carries the horizon colour down into the island's own haze so the gradient is continuous through the horizon line, `glow_bloom` is 0.05, `fog_sky_affect` is 0.10, and each island designs its own sky rather than deriving one from the ambient palette by a ten percent lerp.
The five differ in the *shape* of the gradient as well as the colour: the arctic's is flat and pale top to bottom, the desert's holds deep blue until a hard band of glare at the horizon.
The sky also goes over to night across a break now, which it never did.

**The camera.** Even with all that, the sun was unreachable, because this camera orbits rather than tilts: dragging down walked it round and under the island and never turned the view toward the sky.
So the orbit now stops just above level and everything past that tilts the aim upward from where the camera already is.
Keep dragging and the island slides off the bottom of the frame and you are looking at the weather.
Verified with captures of both bodies. Added a `--pitch=` capture argument, without which none of this could be checked from the command line, which is most of why it went unnoticed.

## The bodies

The previous pass was a parameter sweep: the same capsule torso, ball head, cone ears cut shorter and cone hat swapped for cone horns, at different scales and colours.
The owner was right to call it out.

Rebuilt as two separate functions with no shared shape language.

A hobbit is a pear: narrow at the shoulders, wide and low at the waistcoat, short-legged, with a mass of seven to ten overlapping curls that is most of the head's outline, round flattened ears pressed to the skull, big bare feet with three toes, and buttons up the front.
The hat is now rare and soft when it appears, because a tall pointed hat was doing the work of saying "small fantasy person" and saying the wrong one.

A troll is a cliff: four faceted lumps that do not line up, one shoulder higher than the other, a head wider than it is tall, hands out of proportion to the arms.
Low segment counts everywhere so the flats show, which is the same language the terrain is built in and the only honest way to say "rock" in a project that has refused textures everywhere else.

Two rounds of correction on captures.
The first troll was half again as big and read as a boulder with a pick in it: no head, no shoulders, nothing to read a creature from.
Then both species had their legs swallowed, because `_capsule` adds `radius * 2` to whatever height it is given, so every "belt" in this file was actually a sphere.
Invisible while the torso was a tube of the same width; a problem the moment the legs got short enough to be worth seeing.
Belts and the troll's waist wrap are cylinders now.

# The pivot, built (second session, 2026-08-02)

`DESIGN-one-world.md` said zoom-out-is-the-map was the first thing to build.
It is built, along with everything underneath it that had to exist first.

## The geography

`scripts/region.gd` is the whole pivot in one table: five origins in one world, and the reasoning for each number.

The arc bows rather than sloping.
A line of five places at evenly increasing depth draws a diagonal stripe across the frame with a corner of empty sky above it; a curve with the ends further off and the middle nearest fills a landscape frame symmetrically, reads as a bay rather than a row, and puts home in the foreground where it belongs.
Confirmed on captures both ways round.

East of the Meadow is warm and gets warmer - the Shore, then the Dunes.
West is wet and gets colder - the Green, then the Ice.
That ordering is the supply chain rather than decoration: putting the Ice behind the Green means the only sane way to settle it is to have the Green cutting lumber first and carry it, which is the interaction five separate islands could never have.

Thirty units between neighbours.
Twenty was tried first and was wrong twice over.
Five units of gap put the neighbours in the frame at close zoom as huge pale slabs jammed against the left and right edges, which is not a world with more in it, it is a diorama with clutter round it; and two shores five units apart are not somewhere you walk to, they are somewhere you step to.
At thirty there is more than an island's width of open ground between one shoreline and the next, and a crossing is about seventy-five seconds of hobbit.

**No sea, deliberately, and not yet settled as a question.**
The regions sit in the same luminous haze one island always sat in, because the app has always drawn a floating diorama and five of them in an arc is the same picture rather than a different one.
Water under the world would change the close-up view, which is tuned, and it should be decided on purpose rather than as a side effect of laying out a map.

## The land came out of the island

`scripts/land.gd`.
Height, colour, coastline and mesh were all instance methods on `ElfWorld`, which was fine until there was more than one region on screen and four of them had no elves, no queue and no reason to be an `ElfWorld` at all.
`ElfWorld._ground` and `_on` are now two-line delegates.

`scripts/country.gd` draws those four.
Scenery only - no tick, ever.
The rule that only what is being watched moves is not softened by the world getting bigger, it is what makes a bigger world affordable.

## Zoom out is the map

The picker is deleted: the five chips, the drawn houses at their real build stage, `Plot`, the `chose` signal, all of it.
It was the best-drawn thing in the app and it was answering a question that should never have been asked.
Its one useful line - "They only build while the phone is still." - moved to the title rather than dying with it.

The map is not a screen and there is no transition to get wrong.
It is one number, `_map`, read off the pinch, and everything else follows it: the aim slides from the region to the middle of the world, the haze thins, the camera leans a little further over, the lens goes long, and tapping a region travels to it.

Three things had to be got right and each took a capture to see:

**Distance is no longer proportional to zoom.**
A world this wide would need a thirty-to-one zoom range, and a pinch gets you about four-to-one in one gesture, so opening the map would have taken six of them in a row.
Past `MAP_FROM` the camera heads for wherever the whole world happens to fit, so the width of the world costs the same flick of two fingers whatever is in it.

**A long lens, further back.**
The same argument `ElfWorld` already makes about framing one island.
At thirty-four degrees the frame is sixty-six degrees across, the arc fills nearly all of it, and the two end regions sat right out where rectilinear projection stretches hardest - the Ice and the Dunes were drawn about forty per cent larger than the Meadow despite being further away, which reads as the world bulging at the ends.
Twenty-two degrees from a hundred and seventy metres out is the same picture without it.

**Tapping is unprojection, not hit rectangles.**
Everything on the map is a real thing in the world at its real distance, so asking the camera where it landed is both the simplest way and the only one that cannot drift.

## Settlement

`Progress` gained the one fact that belongs to the world rather than to a place in it: which regions have been settled.
Saves from before the pivot migrate - every island with progress in it is somewhere a person spent hours, and those are settled regions now.

`Land` only wears paths into a settled region, so from the map a place somebody lives reads differently from a place nobody has been, without a pin, a label or a progress ring.

Arriving settles a region instantly.
That is a placeholder for the lantern walk and is marked as one in `_enter`.

## The ground, twice corrected

The owner: *"all I see now is just a land with a lot of big triangles, that is hard to watch."*

It was a perfectly regular lattice, every quad split corner to corner the same way, every facet given its own random colour shift of up to three and a half per cent.
That is a mesh you can see through the render: countable triangles, one direction of diagonal across the whole region, and salt-and-pepper on top making the ground look soiled rather than varied.

First attempt smoothed it completely - normals off the height field, colour per vertex.
The owner, correctly: *"way too shiny, way too un-poly, I told you to un-poly it, you made it too un-poly."*
Shade a coarse mesh off the field alone and the land stops being made of anything. It reads as wax.

The third thing is what shipped.
Every triangle still has its own normal, so the ground is visibly built out of planes, but that normal is bent seventy-two per cent of the way toward the smooth field normal before use, which takes the hard crease off every shared edge.
You can see the land is faceted; you cannot pick out where one facet stops.
`Land.SOFTEN` is the entire dial.

With it:
- the vertices wander off their lattice points by up to just under half a cell, from a hash of their own indices, so no two facets are the same shape and the region is the same shape every time;
- the diagonals alternate;
- every vertex past the shoreline is folded back onto it, which turns a rectangle with its corners hanging off into a closed wandering coastline - much the biggest single improvement to how a region reads from the map;
- the patch colour runs on three waves at frequencies that do not divide into each other and on axes that are not the grid's, because `sin(x) * cos(z)` is a plaid that lines its light and dark up with the mesh;
- the facet noise is gone, replaced by a drift.

**Specular disabled outright.** Roughness was already at one, which is as matte as a rough surface gets, but that is not the same as no highlight - a fully rough dielectric still returns a broad low sheen, and across a hillside under a strong key that sheen is what read as waxed.

## The background was paper

Not related to the pivot, fixed on the way past because it is two thirds of every frame.

The camera looks down from thirty-six degrees through a thirty-four degree lens, so everything behind the land is between nineteen and fifty-three degrees below the horizon, and every pixel of it is the *ground* half of the procedural sky.
At `ground_curve = 0.32` that whole band came out as one flat pale wall: it holds the horizon colour nearly all the way down, which means `ground_bottom_color` had been carefully chosen for a part of the sphere nobody has ever seen.

The parameter runs backwards from how it reads - **lower is a faster fall to the bottom colour**.
Established by rendering the two ends in flat red and blue and looking, which took two captures and is written into the comment so nobody derives it again.
At 0.13 the transition sits inside the visible band and the background graduates from the warm glow near the horizon down into the region's own haze.

Worth knowing before touching it: ambient is 78 per cent sky, so those two colours light the island as well as sitting behind it.

## Roads that wear in

`DESIGN-one-world.md` calls this the most satisfying single item on its list.
It was also the most dishonest thing in the app.

`Land.trodden` drew seven authored routes out from the site with a fixed width and burnt them into the ground colour when a region was built.
The same paths, at full strength, from the first second, in every region - including four nobody had ever set foot in.
It looked right in a screenshot and it meant nothing.

`scripts/wear.gd` records where they actually walk.
One byte per patch of ground, ninety-six by sixty-four over a region, which is a cell about eighteen centimetres across against a person a bit over half a metre tall - roughly a footstep.
Deposited per metre travelled, so somebody standing still does not grind a hole where they are standing, and multiplied up by load, so the routes that wear deepest are the hauling routes, which are the ones the week was actually about.

**It only goes one way.**
Real paths fade and it was tempting.
It is the wrong call for the same reason a disturbance never takes finished work off the queue: against a build that takes a week, anything that quietly undoes an evening teaches people not to open the app.

This is the closest thing the app has to a thesis statement made out of code.
There is no session count, no streak, no graph and no total.
There is a hillside with tracks on it that were not there on Monday, and if Tuesday was spent watching them fetch stone then the track to the quarry is the deepest one.
It cannot be gamed, because the only way to make a mark on it is to have actually put the phone down and watched.

### How it draws

Paths cannot be baked into the mesh - they change while you are watching, and rebuilding twenty thousand vertices every few seconds to move a shade of brown is absurd.
So the ground moved from `StandardMaterial3D` to `scripts/ground.gdshader`: vertex colour for everything the land is, one small texture for where it has been walked, linear-filtered, uploaded twice a second and only when something changed.
The flush returns whether it did anything, and that is what marks the world dirty - walking is progress here, and a session spent watching them haul has to survive being put down even if no work happened to finish in it.

### Two numbers, both found on captures

Tuned by multiplying the deposit rate by forty and running for two hundred seconds, which is how you look at two hours of walking without waiting two hours.

The first pass turned the entire middle of the region into one flat brown field with no structure left in it.
The fault was not the rate, it was `depth`: at 0.82 a fully worn cell went almost entirely to bare earth, and a busy afternoon takes a lot of cells there.
At 0.60 the yard reads as scuffed and the ridges and corners stay green, which is what a building site actually looks like.

`TREAD` shipped at 0.030: a route in steady use is showing after about twenty minutes of being watched and is a proper track by the second afternoon.
Deliberately not faster. A path that appears in five minutes is a feature; a path that took a week is a record.

### Everything downstream of it

- Six kilobytes a region in the save, base64'd, because a `PackedByteArray` in a `ConfigFile` is written out as six thousand comma-separated decimals.
- The wear is loaded *before* `_build_clutter`, so grass and scrub go in wherever the paths were not. A region lived in for a week comes back with its verges pushed off the tracks; a fresh one comes back with grass over everything. Verified on a capture three seconds into a second launch.
- `Country` loads each far region's saved wear too, so from the map a place somebody has spent a week in is visibly scuffed and one nobody has been to is clean. That is the map's only marker, and it is not a marker - it is the ground.

## The world is joined up now

Started on the lantern - the walk that settles a new region - and got about four lines in before hitting something that should have been obvious from the first map capture and was not: **there is nothing to walk on between the regions.**

Five plates floating in haze is not a world you can spread across.
It is five islands you can be teleported between, which is the exact thing this direction replaced.
And it looks like it: the map was reading as the old design even though the code underneath it was the new one.

`scripts/causeway.gd` is the answer.
A neck of ground between each joined pair in `Region.NEIGHBOURS`, sagging in the middle, crowned across its width, falling away at the sides exactly as the shoreline does, with each end buried a little way into the land it leaves so it beds in instead of butting up against it.

Not bridges.
Nothing built, no deck, no rail, no piling - a spit of bare rock and sand of the kind that joins a headland to an island at low water.
The app has refused every opportunity to draw architecture it did not have to draw and this was not the place to start.

**The colour is the best part and it was free.**
A neck runs from one climate's shore to the next one's and is coloured by interpolating between them, so the world grades into itself rather than being five swatches on a table.
The Ice into the Green goes grey shingle to wet dark earth; the Shore into the Dunes bleaches out as it goes.
One lerp.

Three passes to get the proportion:
- At three units across against a thirty unit gap they were two pixels on the map and read as wires strung between plates.
- At six they read as ramps and dominated the regions they joined.
- At four and a half, with the sag halved and some length-wise wobble in the spine, they read as isthmuses. At close zoom they sweep off the left and right of the frame as ways out of the region, which turns out to be worth having on its own - it says there is more world that way.
- Tone knocked back toward earth and darkened a tenth, because straight shore colour came out brighter than the shores it joined and made the necks the lightest thing in the frame.

### An hour lost to winding

The necks rendered as nothing at all. Not dark, not misshapen - absent.

That is what a mesh that was never built looks like, so the search went to the vertex data, the AABB, the surface count, the material, the node's visibility and its render layers, all of which were fine, and then to dropping a plain white box at the same coordinates, which rendered immediately.
It was back-face culling the whole time.

Godot's front face is the one whose edge cross product points *away* from the viewer, so ground seen from above wants `cross(e1, e2)` pointing down.
Going round a quad the natural way here - across the width first, then along the run - gives the opposite sign from the way `Land` goes round its grid.
Written into the source at the point of use, because the failure mode gives no clue whatsoever what it is.

## The lantern

Expansion, for real. A region opens when somebody walks a light to it.

Not a button, not a purchase, not a threshold crossed on a counter: one hobbit, thirty metres of open ground, and about eighty seconds of somebody's whole attention each way.
The site is a pair of hands short for the whole of it and nowhere does the app say so in a number.

It is also the rule at its sharpest.
Every other job on the island sits somewhere in a cycle that a disturbance merely delays.
This one is a single unbroken errand across open ground, and the carrier **stops dead where they stand and waits, holding the light**, until the phone is put back down.
They do not walk through the quarter-hour break either - verified by accident, on a save that happened to restore mid-break, where the carrier stood on the causeway for the whole of it.

### The architecture, which was the whole decision

The obvious build is a separate walker: a figure that is not an `Elf`, living outside `ElfWorld`, crossing between regions with its own animation.
That would have meant either duplicating two hundred lines of gait, gaze, mood and stride, or shipping a sliding mannequin - and the one thing this whole app is built to protect is that these read as somebody rather than as a token.

So instead **the causeway became ground.**
`Causeway` is an object now rather than a mesh-building function: it answers `height` for any point, and `ElfWorld._ground` takes whichever is higher, its own land or a neck.
An elf can then simply walk onto it.

The carrier is an ordinary resident with an unusual errand - `Task.CARRY_FIRE`, appended to the enum rather than slotted in, because those values are the keys of every elf's saved `affinity` and renumbering them would hand a week of formed specialisms to the wrong jobs.
Everything else applies unchanged: they hesitate, they look about, they slow going uphill, and they get chosen by who happens to have empty hands.

The mesh is now built off exactly the same `spine` and `height` the feet read, rather than a second copy of the shape.

### Three bugs, all of them the same bug

Each one was some version of *the ground is not where the walking is*.

**The deadlock.** Getting onto the neck was gated on comparing the neck's height to the land's. But the first stretch of a neck is deliberately buried in the region it leaves, so down there the land is legitimately higher and the test says "not on it" forever. The first carrier walked to a metre from the shore and marched on the spot indefinitely. Now tested by distance from the spine.

**The sinking.** `_ground` learned about causeways; `_on` did not, and still went straight to `Land`. Every foot, shadow and anchor is placed through `_on`. Fixed, and the reason is written above the function.

**The nine-metre drop.** The carrier reached the far end of the neck, stepped off it onto the destination region - which `ElfWorld` did not know existed - and sank into the haze, still walking, all the way to the far hearth. A world now also holds the `Land` of its neighbours. Then the same thing again on the way home, because the return leg was a straight line to the home hearth that left the causeway at both ends; the route walks the spine in tenths in both directions now.

Every one of these was found by running the sim for four minutes with a position trace, which was much faster than reasoning about it.

### What it looks like

The lantern is the smallest object in the app and by a distance the brightest - a box of glass past white so it clips the glow threshold, on an iron bail below the grip so the existing arm swing carries it, with an `OmniLight3D` inside.
Across thirty metres of unlit ground you do not see a hobbit, you see a light going somewhere, and that is the better picture.

When it arrives the far hearth lights and stays lit.
On the map that is now the difference between a region somebody lives in and one nobody has been to: a coloured shape with tracks worn across it and one warm point on it, against a coloured shape.
No pin, no label, no ring - and it is the same fire the lantern was carried there to light.

### Kept

- `--fire=<region>` on the command line, alongside `--rest=`, because eighty seconds of somebody walking is not something you want to have to tap for every time you change a number.
- The journey is saved per region. Close the app halfway across and the walk starts again from the hearth rather than resuming mid-neck: nobody's position is saved and inventing one would be worse than repeating a minute. Nothing that was finished is lost - the region simply is not settled yet, which is exactly what was true when you put it down.

## Four changes decided, none of them built

Written up as `handoffs/NEXT-SESSION-feeding-and-locks.md` at the owner's request: plan only, no code.

**The stillness rule goes entirely.** Not just the population loss - the accelerometer stops being an input at all. `charge.gd` deleted, `_rattled` and its seven uses gone, `_shrink`, `disturbed`, `_quake`. The tilt parallax survives because it is a separate mechanism. The work and rest cycle survives because it never depended on the accelerometer.

The one part that is not deletion: nothing sets the crew size afterwards. Recommended a full crew that still walks in one at a time, rather than reintroducing a growth curve on a number.

**A two-hour feed you tap to rain food**, which they eat - cooking the fish, taking the bread and fruit straight - and then work at three times speed for ten minutes. On app-open time only, confirmed. Food deliberately kept out of `Plan.Kind`, because those sixteen are building materials with bills attached and adding food to that enum drags it into the queue economy, the belief system and the save format.

**Camera bounds dropped.** Ten minutes of work.

**Locked regions.** The Shore and the Green stay lantern-settled; the Dunes and the Ice are paid and carry a white padlock drawn in screen space. Flagged clearly: there is no in-app purchase machinery in this project at all, Godot ships no StoreKit support, and that piece is its own session rather than part of this one.

Three of the four reverse decisions the project's own documents list as not worth relitigating, and one contradicts the business model in `DESIGN-one-world.md`. That is recorded in a table at the top of the handoff, not to argue it, but because a handoff that stays silent produces a future session that "fixes" the code back to match the docs. The first task of that session is to correct `HANDOFF.md`, before any code moves.

Amended shortly after: the feed's progress indicator is not a circular bar in the corner.
The owner's replacement is much better - **a light travels round the region's own coastline over the two hours, and when it closes the circuit the whole outline breathes.**

It keeps the app at zero HUD, which is the thing it has spent its whole life refusing to give up; the only overlay in the product remains one `Label` reading "Turn" or "Move".
It is non-numeric by construction - there is nothing to read and no way to express it as a percentage.
And it uses the closed wandering shoreline the terrain work made real earlier the same day, which was built for the map's sake and now pays off twice.

Two things written into the plan because they will not behave as expected.
The glow will never be seen moving: two hours around a forty-unit perimeter is half a centimetre a second, so the head has to be legible by position rather than by motion.
And the Ice will fight it, because a warm ember glow on a pale blue-white coast is far weaker than the same glow on the Meadow, so it probably wants a per-biome accent in the same way each region already designs its own sky.

## A hundred houses, which should be a grammar

Owner's proposal at the end of the day, written into `NEXT-SESSION-what-the-other-sites-build.md` because it answers most of what that file was written to ask.

A hundred different houses, no two alike; the variant drawn at random from within the region's own style; and - the decision that changes the shape of everything - **a region grows a village rather than one house.**

Three things went in alongside it.

**Do not author a hundred houses.**
A hundred hand-written queues at `plan.gd` scale is fifty thousand works.
Five footprints, four storey counts, four roof forms, five cladding schemes and three window rhythms is twenty-one authored pieces and twelve hundred combinations, constrained per region and drawn without replacement.
More variety than a hundred for about a sixtieth of the work, and the only version that is buildable.
The variety has to come from massing, pitch, palette and window rhythm rather than textures, because there is not one texture anywhere in this project.

**Villages are what make the numbers work, and `plan.gd` already knows why.**
Ten houses a region at a week each is a year.
But the ten workshops and six machines are one-time per region, so the first house costs a week including the infrastructure and the rest cost two or three days.
A village of six is about three weeks a region and the world is three or four months - long, finishable, and a village that visibly accelerates as it grows, which is the right feeling for a settlement taking hold.

**The save format is what will bite.**
`Progress` stores `done` as indices into the queue, and those indices only mean anything relative to the queue that produced them.
A per-house seed has to be saved with the site and the queue rebuilt from it before `done` is applied, with a generator version alongside - otherwise the first time anybody adjusts the grammar, every existing save silently reconstructs the wrong building without erroring.

The best part of the direction is free: several plots per region means the paths between them cross, and the ground already records where people walk, so **a village wears its own street plan into the terrain without anybody authoring one.**

## One band, and settlers who never leave

Asked the owner three questions at the end of the day. The answer to the second was better than any of the three options offered, and it resolves more than it was asked to.

**There is one band of builders and it is the same band all game.** They travel, and when a region is finished they move to one that is not. **Each region also keeps its own people, permanently** - they arrive as the village fills and they stay for the rest of the world's life. Two populations: about twelve who build and travel, and a growing number who live and never leave.

Three things fall out of it that were open problems ten minutes earlier.

It **answers what sets the crew size** once the stillness rule and `charge.gd` are gone. The band is the crew. Fixed roster, no curve, nothing to optimise.

It **removes the second of the two risks `DESIGN-one-world.md` names.** That document worried about attention splitting across a bigger world. With one travelling band there is only ever one place being built, so it cannot split. The risk does not need managing, it stops existing.

It **gives "finished" something to look like** - smoke, lit windows, people fishing, no scaffolding and no crew. The only place in the app where nothing is being worked on, and that being the point.

The migration is going to be the set piece: the lantern is one person carrying a light, and this is the whole band crossing a neck of land in a line, on exactly the same machinery. Everything for it exists already.

Left in the doc as the most promising thread and on nobody's critical path: an old mason stepping out of the band into a house on the region they framed, replaced by somebody young. That is the generations idea in one image, and you would meet them again every time you passed through.

The other two answers: the feed stays at 3x for ten minutes, deliberately, having been told what it does to the week. And leaving the app running is legitimate play now - app-open time is the currency. Recorded with two consequences a fresh reader should not have to derive: the project sets no `keep_screen_on` override so Godot's default of true applies and the screen never sleeps, and the "no energy timers, no skip-the-wait" marketing line cannot ship as written alongside a two-hour boost and paid regions.
