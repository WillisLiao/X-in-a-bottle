# 2026-07-31 - Bottle becomes Elvle

The app stopped being four environments with elves in one of them and became one
environment with five islands in it.

## What changed, in order of how much it matters

### The product

Lightning, Tree and Ice are gone - scripts deleted, not disabled.
The app is Elvle now: `config/name`, both bundle ids (`com.lull.elvle`), the
export paths and `deploy.sh` all follow.
"Elvle" rather than "Elves in a Bottle" because the long name truncates on the
iOS home screen, and the subtitle carries the full one on the title card.

**Touching the screen no longer costs anything.**
This is the biggest single change to the mechanism and it needs defending.
The old rule charged for every tap and swipe on the reasoning that handling the
phone is handling the phone.
That was the right instinct and the wrong rule: it charged the user for turning
the world round to see what was happening on the far side, which is punishing
attention in an app whose entire subject is attention.
The cost is now movement alone, on the accelerometer.
A phone flat on a desk with a finger turning the island is perfectly still and
free; a phone picked up is not.
That is also the honest version - what breaks a stretch of work is picking the
thing up, not looking at it.

Which freed the gestures.
Drag orbits, pinch zooms between 0.42 and 1.65 of nominal, and tilt still adds
parallax on top of wherever the user has put the camera.

### The build takes a week

One three-storey house per island, and it is not an animation reaching a hundred
per cent.
`Plan.works()` is a queue of **498 works** in the order a house is actually
built, and everything in it is the same type: a limekiln, a treadwheel crane, a
floor joist and a doorknob are all somewhere on the island with a bill of
materials and a piece of geometry that appears when the bill is met.

Order *is* the tech tree.
There is no unlock table and no dependency graph, because a building is not a
graph - you cannot pour a footing before you have dug for it, and you cannot dig
before somebody has built the thing that digs.
Expressing it as a queue means it cannot deadlock and cannot be solved out of
sequence, and it means the machines simply sit in the line at the point they
first become necessary.

Sixteen materials: six dug, ten made, every recipe taking at least two units in
for one out.
Ten workshops the elves have to build before they can use them, six pieces of
equipment.

The brief named excavators, concrete pump trucks, boom cranes and dumpsters.
Those are the right functions and they are all in there, but a diesel bobcat on
an island of thirty-centimetre elves reads as a toy dropped into the scene, so
each is built as the machine that does that job at their scale and in their
century: a hand-cranked bucket dredge, a piston pump on a barrel, a treadwheel
crane, a tipping cart.
The construction sequence is real; the machinery is theirs.

The last third of the queue is deliberately theatrical.
Three elevations close up and the fourth - the one facing the camera - is held
back, so the whole interior fit-out is a dollhouse: you watch the ducting,
plumbing, insulation, plaster, stairs and cabinets go in through an open wall.
Then the front goes on, the windows go in, and the lights come on behind them.

Measured: 1664 units delivered across the queue, 1448 of them made rather than
dug, about 11,000 acts of work, which is 78 elf-hours.
`Plan.EFFORT` multiplies every bill and is the one dial.

### Five islands, each with its own build

Meadow, Ice, Dunes, Shore, Green.
They share the layout, because it is tuned - shortest errand two and a half
metres, longest nearly nine, the ridge between the yard and the reedbed so an
elf sent for reed walks out of sight - and re-tuning it five times would produce
five worse versions of it.

What differs is the palette, the relief, the trees, and **what the ground will
give you**.
The Ice is slow at everything and has almost no timber; the Dunes give sand for
nothing so glass and concrete race ahead of a frame nobody can build; the Shore
is rich in reed and sand and a long way from iron; the Green pours out timber
and fights you for stone.
Same blueprint, five different arguments about what to do next.

### It survives being put down

`Progress` keeps four things per island in `user://elvle.cfg`: which works are
finished, how much of each material is lying about, cumulative focus, and the
residents.

There is deliberately no offline progress and there will not be.
The only thing that moves this app forward is a phone lying still with the
screen on, and an elf who laid a joist while you were asleep would be a lie
about what you did.

The residents are the nice one.
Every elf is generated from a single integer, so keeping the integers brings
back the same people - the tall one with the crooked hat who always ends up at
the kiln is the same tall one on Saturday - and their formed affinities come
with them.
Recognising somebody is most of believing in them, and this is the cheapest
interiority in the app.

### The menu

Two screens, four words of copy, one control.
Not a level select: no thumbnails, no stars, no percentages, no locked islands.
A percentage would be the most damaging thing that could go on that screen,
because the moment progress is a number somebody optimises it, and the only way
to optimise anything here is to leave the phone alone longer than you meant to.

Each island is one chip of its own ground - the real terrain colours the world
is built from - with a black house on it at the stage that island's house is
actually at: staked ground, then a footing, then open framing, then a topped-out
skeleton with rafters, then something clad, then lit windows.
The stage thresholds are read off the real queue via `Plan.fraction_after`, so
adding a course of tiles cannot put the drawing and the build out of step.
Identity and progress are the same mark, which is one mark rather than two.

The house reaches full height when the frame tops out, a third of the way
through, and everything after that is roof, walls, windows and light.
That is the shape of a real build and it is why a progress bar would have been
the wrong picture.

## Three bugs worth writing down

**Vertex colours are consumed as linear.**
Every colour in this project is written in sRGB, and the terrain passes them
straight to `vertex_color_use_as_albedo`.
That lifts mid-tones by about seventy per cent, so the meadow rendered as pale
sage and the desert as bare white paper.
It looks exactly like a lighting problem and it is a colour-space one.
`.srgb_to_linear()` in `_face`.

**A mixed input heap can be monopolised and deadlock permanently.**
The mixer eats lime, sand and stone.
Elves haul on what they *believe* is in a heap rather than what is there, so
several of them independently decided it needed sand, and it ended up with eight
sand and no room for anything else - forever.
The belief system working exactly as designed, producing a hard deadlock.

The fix is not to let them read the real number.
It is a per-kind ceiling on the heap plus a `_drop` that discovers the heap is
full on arrival and takes the load somewhere else, which is precisely the
behaviour the beliefs are *for*: being wrong, finding out, and dealing with it.

**Delivery read a node that did not exist yet.**
`_deliver` noted `work.holder.global_position`, but a work has no holder until it
is finished.
Every delivery threw before it ran `_finish`, so no work was ever completed and
the island looked alive while achieving nothing.
Three minutes of screen capture did not show this; a headless harness found it in
one run.

## On the harness

`sim.gd` runs an island headless at thirty ticks a second, far faster than real
time, and prints what the elves actually spent the day doing plus the contents of
every heap.
It found all three bugs above within about ten minutes, after real-time captures
had hidden them - including the delivery bug, which meant *no work was ever
completed* while the island looked perfectly alive.

It started as scratch and is being kept, because tuning an economy by watching
screenshots does not work.

```sh
Godot --path . --headless --script sim.gd -- --minutes=90 --island=0
```

The tuning it produced is worth keeping.
The island doubled in size and the distance penalty did not, so the quarry scored
below sitting down and a full crew spent its day at the fire: 36% idle, 20%
looking around, 29 works in twenty minutes and then permanent stall.
Distance is now 4.2p a metre rather than 8.5p, the "stick with what you were
doing" bonus no longer applies to idling, fishing and wandering off, and the
loafing floor came down.

Ninety simulated minutes with a permanent full crew of twelve, after tuning:

```
haul 32.8%  gather 15.5%  craft 12.1%  deliver 6.9%  fit 1.3%   = 69% working
rest 13.8%  idle 11.8%  own 2.5%  look 1.8%  play 1.2%
133 works of 498, now framing storey two and short of cloth for the crane
```

That is 27% of the queue in ninety minutes, but the early works are much the
cheapest, so cost-weighted it is nearer a fifth: call it seven or eight hours at
a crew that never drops below twelve.
A real crew only reaches twelve after fifteen unbroken minutes and loses a third
of itself every time the phone moves, so ten to fourteen hours of held stillness
is the honest figure - five to ten days at an hour or two a day.
`EFFORT` stays at 1.0.

Two numbers to keep an eye on.
`play` at 1.2% is lower than it should be; somebody should always be at the
water.
`look` at 1.8% is low but defensible - they refresh beliefs constantly while
hauling, so the deliberate go-and-check only wins when there is nothing better,
which is the right shape. If it hits zero, the belief system has stopped
producing the behaviour it exists for.

## Still rough

- The elves have relationships and visible handoffs now, but nobody has watched
  one for ninety seconds and been asked to describe it. That test has not been
  run against this build.
- Nothing has been on the device. The phone-runs-warm question is still open and
  the island is bigger and busier than the thing that made it warm.
- The near shore still reaches the bottom edge of the frame. Acceptable, and it
  fills in as the island builds up, but it is not composed.
- No sound at all.
